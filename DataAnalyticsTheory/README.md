# 1. Introduction to Data Analytics

## What is Data?

**Data** is a collection of facts, observations, measurements, records, or information that can be stored and analyzed.

Examples:

```text
Customer Name
Age
Salary
Product Price
Number of Orders
Transaction Date
Website Visits
```

A single value can be data:

```text
25
```

A collection of values can also be data:

```text
25, 30, 28, 42, 35
```

---

# What is Analytics?

**Analytics** is the systematic process of examining data to discover useful information, patterns, relationships, and insights.

In simple terms:

```text
Data
 ↓
Examine
 ↓
Understand
 ↓
Find Patterns
 ↓
Generate Insights
 ↓
Make Decisions
```

---

# What is Data Analytics?

**Data Analytics is the process of collecting, cleaning, transforming, analyzing, and interpreting data to discover useful insights and support decision-making.**

The main purpose is not simply to calculate numbers.

The purpose is to answer questions and support decisions.

Example:

```text
Raw Data
   ↓
10,000 transactions
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Find sales declining in Region A
   ↓
Investigate
   ↓
Product X sales dropped
   ↓
Business Action
   ↓
Investigate pricing / competition / availability
```

---

# Why is Data Analytics Important?

Organizations generate huge amounts of data.

Without analytics:

```text
Large Data
    ↓
Difficult to understand
    ↓
Difficult to make decisions
```

With analytics:

```text
Large Data
    ↓
Analysis
    ↓
Patterns
    ↓
Insights
    ↓
Decisions
```

Analytics helps organizations:

* understand customers
* increase revenue
* reduce costs
* improve products
* identify risks
* optimize operations
* measure performance
* forecast future trends
* detect problems
* make evidence-based decisions

---

# Objectives of Data Analytics

The major objectives are:

1. Understand what happened
2. Identify why it happened
3. Predict what may happen
4. Determine what should be done
5. Support decision-making
6. Improve business performance
7. Reduce uncertainty
8. Identify opportunities
9. Identify risks
10. Optimize resources

# Data Analytics vs Data Analysis

These terms are often used interchangeably, but there is a useful distinction.

### Data Analysis

Data analysis generally refers to examining data to answer a specific question or understand a dataset.

### Data Analytics

Data analytics is broader and can include:

```text
Data Collection
Data Preparation
Data Analysis
Visualization
Interpretation
Communication
Decision Making
```

---

# Data Analytics vs Data Science

| Data Analytics                                 | Data Science                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Focuses heavily on understanding existing data | Broader field                      |
| Reporting and insights                         | Analytics + ML + advanced modeling |
| Dashboards                                     | Predictive models                  |
| SQL                                            | Python/R                           |
| Statistics                                     | Statistics + ML                    |
| Business questions                             | Business + scientific problems     |

There is significant overlap between the two fields.

---

# Data Analytics vs Business Analytics

**Data Analytics** focuses on extracting insights from data.

**Business Analytics** specifically applies analytical techniques to business problems.

Example:

```text
Data Analytics:
Analyze customer purchase patterns.

Business Analytics:
Determine which customer segment should receive a promotional campaign.
```

---

# Data Analytics vs Business Intelligence

## Business Intelligence

* reporting
* dashboards
* KPIs
* monitoring business performance

## Data Analytics

Analytics goes further into:

* exploration
* statistical analysis
* investigation
* experimentation
* prediction
* deeper insights

# 3. Measurement Scales

Measurement scales determine how variables can be interpreted and analyzed.

There are four major scales:

```text
Nominal
Ordinal
Interval
Ratio
```

---

# Nominal Scale

Categories without meaningful order.

Examples:

```text
Gender
City
Color
Department
Product Category
```

Example:

```text
Red
Blue
Green
```

There is no natural ranking.

---

# Ordinal Scale

Categories with meaningful order.

Example:

```text
Poor
Average
Good
Excellent
```

Order exists, but the difference between categories is not necessarily equal.

---

# Interval Scale

Values have meaningful differences but no true zero.

Example:

```text
Temperature in Celsius
```

The difference between 20°C and 30°C is meaningful.

But 0°C does not mean "no temperature."

---

# Ratio Scale

Has:

* meaningful differences
* meaningful zero

Examples:

```text
Age
Weight
Height
Revenue
Salary
Distance
```

For example:

```text
₹20,000 is twice ₹10,000
```

# 14. Exploratory Data Analysis

**EDA = Exploratory Data Analysis**

EDA is the systematic investigation of a dataset to understand its:

* structure
* distributions
* relationships
* patterns
* anomalies
* trends

EDA is usually performed before formal conclusions or modeling.

---

# Objectives of EDA

EDA helps answer:

```text
What does the data look like?
What variables exist?
Are there missing values?
Are there outliers?
How are variables distributed?
Which variables are related?
Are there unusual patterns?
```

---

# Univariate Analysis

Analyzes one variable.

Example:

```text

```

* What is the average?
* What is the distribution?
* Are there outliers?

---

# Bivariate Analysis

Analyzes two variables.

Example:

```text

```

Used to understand relationships.

---

# Multivariate Analysis

```text

```

Arithmetic average.

```text
Mean = Sum of values / Number of values
```

---

# Median

The middle value after sorting.

Useful when data contains extreme values.

---

# Mode

The most frequently occurring value.

---

# Measures of Dispersion

Describe how spread out data is.

Important measures:

```text
Range
Variance
Standard Deviation
IQR
```

---

# Range

```text
Maximum - Minimum
```

---

# Variance

Measures the average squared deviation from the mean.

Higher variance means greater dispersion.

---

# Standard Deviation

Square root of variance.

It expresses dispersion in the same units as the original variable.

---

# Percentile

A percentile tells you the position of a value relative to a distribution.

Example:

> A value at the 90th percentile is greater than or equal to approximately 90% of observations.

---

# Quartiles

Divide data into four parts.

```text
Q1 = 25th percentile
Q2 = 50th percentile
Q3 = 75th percentile
```

---

# IQR

Interquartile Range:

```text
IQR = Q3 - Q1
```

A common outlier rule is:

```text
Lower Bound = Q1 - 1.5 × IQR

Upper Bound = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
```

---

# Skewness

Describes asymmetry in a distribution.

```text
Positive Skew
     →
Long right tail

Negative Skew
     ←
Long left tail
```

---

# Kurtosis

Describes aspects of the tail behavior/peakedness of a distribution.

In practical analytics, it can help identify distributions with unusually heavy or light tails.

# 16. Probability

Probability measures the likelihood of an event.

```text
0 ≤ Probability ≤ 1
```

Where:

```text
0 = Impossible
1 = Certain
```

---

# Conditional Probability

Probability of event A given event B.

Conceptually:

```text
P(A | B)
```

Example:

> Probability that a customer purchases given that they clicked an advertisement.

---

# Independent Events

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.

---

# Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem describes how a prior belief is updated using new evidence.

Conceptually:

```text
Posterior
=
Likelihood × Prior
------------------
Evidence
```

It is important in:

* risk analysis
* classification
* diagnosis
* probabilistic reasoning

---

# Normal Distribution

Bell-shaped distribution.

Characteristics:

* symmetric
* mean = median = mode
* defined by mean and standard deviation

Common in statistical analysis.

---

# Binomial Distribution

Models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials when each trial has two possible outcomes.

Example:

---

# Poisson Distribution

Used for modeling counts of events occurring over a fixed interval under appropriate assumptions.

Examples:

# Uniform Distribution

All values within a defined interval have equal probability density.

# Exponential Distribution

# 18. Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics uses sample data to make conclusions about a population.

Main concepts:

```text
Sampling
Sampling Distribution
Confidence Intervals
Hypothesis Testing
```

---

# Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem states, under common conditions, that the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size becomes sufficiently large.

It is fundamental to statistical inference.

---

# Confidence Interval

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for a population parameter based on sample data and a specified confidence procedure.

Common levels:

```text
90%
95%
99%
```

Important:

A 95% confidence interval does **not** technically mean there is a 95% probability that the fixed parameter lies inside this particular interval.

# 19. Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is a framework for evaluating evidence against a null hypothesis.

Two major hypotheses:

```text
H₀ = Null Hypothesis
H₁ = Alternative Hypothesis
```

---

# Null Hypothesis

Usually represents:

```text
No effect
No difference
No relationship
```

---

# Alternative Hypothesis

Represents:

```text
There is an effect
There is a difference
There is a relationship
```

---

# p-value

The p-value measures how incompatible the observed data (or something more extreme), under the statistical model, is with the null hypothesis.

A small p-value provides evidence against the null hypothesis.

It is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

---

# Significance Level

Common significance level:

```text
α = 0.05
```

A decision rule may be:

```text
p < α
→ Reject H₀

p ≥ α
→ Fail to reject H₀
```

This should be interpreted together with effect size, uncertainty, study design, and practical significance.

---

# Type I Error

Rejecting a true null hypothesis.

```text
False Positive
```

---

# Type II Error

Failing to reject a false null hypothesis.

```text
False Negative
```

---

# Statistical Power

Probability of detecting an effect when a specified effect truly exists, under the chosen design and significance criterion.

Often:

```text
Power = 1 - β
```

where β is the Type II error probability.

---


---

# z-Test

Can be used for certain mean/proportion testing situations when assumptions and known variance conditions support it.

---

# Chi-Square Test

Common applications:


---

# ANOVA

**Analysis of Variance**

Used to compare means across multiple groups.

Example:

```text

Region A
Region B
Region C
```

ANOVA tests whether there is evidence that at least one group mean differs.

```text

 0 → No linear correlation
```

Measures monotonic association using ranked values.

Useful when:

---

This is one of the most important concepts in analytics.

```text

≠
```

# 22. Regression

Regression models relationships between a target variable and one or more explanatory variables.

---

# Simple Linear Regression

One predictor:

```text
Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε
```

Where:

```text
Y  = dependent variable
X  = independent variable
β₀ = intercept
β₁ = coefficient
ε  = error term
```

---

# Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple predictors:

```text
Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + ... + βₙXₙ + ε
```

---

# R²

R-squared measures the proportion of variability in the dependent variable explained by the model, under the model's definition of explained variation.

---

# Adjusted R²

Adjusted R² accounts for the number of predictors and can be useful when comparing models with different numbers of predictors.

---

# Regression Assumptions

Depending on the model and inference goal, common assumptions include:

```text
Linearity
Independence
Homoscedasticity
Appropriate error distribution
Limited problematic multicollinearity
```

# 39. Common Analytics Problems

## Selection Bias

The sample is not representative of the population relevant to the question.

---

# Sampling Bias

Certain groups are systematically overrepresented or underrepresented.

---

# Survivorship Bias

Analysis focuses only on entities that remain visible while ignoring those that disappeared.

Example:

Studying only successful companies while ignoring failed companies.

---

# Confirmation Bias

Looking mainly for evidence supporting an existing belief.

---

# Simpson's Paradox

A relationship observed in multiple groups can reverse or disappear when the groups are combined.

This demonstrates why aggregation and segmentation both matter.

---

# Data Leakage

Information that would not legitimately be available at prediction or decision time accidentally enters an analysis/model.

It can produce misleadingly strong results.

---

# Correlation vs Causation

Never automatically conclude:

```text
A correlates with B
        ↓
A causes B
```

Investigate:

```text
Confounders
Experimental evidence
Temporal order
Alternative explanations
Study design
```

# 🚀 Final Definition to Remember

> **Data Analytics is the systematic process of collecting, preparing, exploring, analyzing, interpreting, and communicating data to generate insights and support better decisions.**

The complete flow is:

```text
DATA
 ↓
COLLECT
 ↓
UNDERSTAND
 ↓
CLEAN
 ↓
PREPROCESS
 ↓
EXPLORE
 ↓
ANALYZE
 ↓
VISUALIZE
 ↓
INTERPRET
 ↓
COMMUNICATE
 ↓
DECIDE
 ↓
MONITOR
```

And the four major analytical questions are:

```text
DESCRIPTIVE
What happened?
        ↓
DIAGNOSTIC
Why did it happen?
        ↓
PREDICTIVE
What might happen?
        ↓
PRESCRIPTIVE
What should we do?
```

This is the **core theoretical foundation of Data Analytics**, from the basic concept of data all the way through statistics, EDA, business analytics, experimentation, advanced analytics, visualization, and decision-making.
